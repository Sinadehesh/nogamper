Most people with a gambling problem do not think they have one, for a long time. Not because they are in denial in some dramatic way, but because the picture in their head of “a gambling addict” is somebody who has lost a house, and they have not lost a house. They have just had a bad month. Again.

There is no line you have to cross before it counts. Gambling harm comes in every size, and the earlier it is noticed the easier it is to turn round. This page sets out what clinicians look for, in plain words, so that you can check it against your own life or that of someone you are worried about.

It is not a diagnosis. Only a professional can give one. It is a way of taking the question seriously.

## Two questions to start with

Researchers looking for the shortest possible screen found that two questions separate people with a serious gambling problem from people without one remarkably well:

1. **Have you ever felt the need to bet more and more money?**
2. **Have you ever had to lie to people important to you about how much you gambled?**

A yes to either is not proof of anything. It is a reason to read the rest of this page slowly, and to be honest while you do.

## The nine signs clinicians use

Psychiatrists diagnose gambling disorder from a list of nine signs. Four or more in the same twelve months meets the definition; the more there are, the more severe it is considered. In everyday language:

1. **Needing bigger stakes** to get the same excitement that smaller ones used to give.
2. **Feeling restless or irritable** when you try to cut down or stop.
3. **Having tried to control it, cut back or stop — more than once — and not managed.**
4. **Thinking about it a great deal:** replaying past bets, planning the next one, working out where the money will come from.
5. **Gambling when you feel bad** — anxious, low, guilty, helpless — as a way of feeling something else.
6. **Chasing:** after losing, going back to try to get even.
7. **Lying** to hide how much you gamble.
8. **Putting something important at risk or losing it** — a relationship, a job, a course, an opportunity — because of gambling.
9. **Relying on other people for money** to get out of a hole that gambling dug.

Fewer than four does not mean there is nothing to worry about. Two or three of these are already costing somebody something.

Notice also what is *not* on the list: the amount. There is no sum of money that defines a problem. Somebody betting small amounts they cannot afford, and lying about it, is in more trouble than somebody betting large amounts they can.

## What it looks like day to day

The clinical list is abstract. This is how the same things tend to show up.

**Money**

- You bet more than you can afford to lose.
- You have borrowed, used credit, sold something or dipped into savings to gamble, or to cover what gambling cost.
- Bills are late that used not to be.
- You do not actually know how much you have lost this year, and you would rather not add it up.

**Time and attention**

- It takes more of your day than you meant it to, and more than you tell people.
- You check odds or results when you are supposed to be doing something else — at work, at dinner, in bed.
- Things you used to enjoy have become less interesting.

**Mood**

- Your mood follows your results. A win makes the day; a loss ruins it.
- You gamble to escape stress, and gambling has become the main source of the stress.
- You feel guilt or dread afterwards, and bet again partly to get away from that.

**Secrecy**

- You hide statements, clear your history, keep a second account, or have an explanation ready.
- You feel defensive when it comes up, out of proportion to what was said.

**Control**

- You set limits and go past them.
- You tell yourself this is the last time, often.
- You have stopped for a while, felt fine, and gone back harder.

## “But it isn't really gambling”

One sign deserves its own heading because it is so common now.

A great deal of gambling no longer looks like gambling. It looks like trading — crypto, options, a prediction market — or like a game with something to buy inside it, or like a harmless accumulator among friends. It comes with charts and jargon and a feeling of skill.

The label does not matter. The pattern does. If you are putting money at risk on outcomes you do not control, doing it more than you intend, hiding it, and going back to recover losses, every item on the list above applies to you just as it would at a roulette table. “I'm investing” is one of the things people say for a long time before they say anything else. ## If you are worried about someone else

You usually cannot see the gambling. You see what it leaves behind:

- money that does not add up — unexplained shortfalls, new debts, requests to borrow with vague reasons;
- secrecy about a phone, an inbox or the post;
- moods that swing for no reason you can see, especially around sport or late at night;
- pulling away from family, friends and things they used to care about;
- defensiveness, or anger, when money is mentioned.

None of these proves anything on its own. Together they are a reason to ask, and how you ask matters. Pick a calm moment. Say what you have noticed and that you are worried, rather than what you think they have done. Expect it to be denied the first time. Do not lend money to cover gambling debts: it feels like help and works like fuel.

And look after yourself. The helplines listed on [our help page](/help) are for families and friends as well, and it is entirely reasonable to call one about somebody else.

## If it feels much worse than a checklist

Gambling problems carry a real risk of suicide; the World Health Organization lists it among the harms. If you are having thoughts of ending your life, that is an emergency, not a footnote. Call your local emergency number or a crisis line now. In the United States, call or text 988. In the United Kingdom and Ireland, call Samaritans on 116 123. Debts can be dealt with, and so can gambling. Both look impossible from the inside and are not.

## What to do next

If some of this is you, three things, in this order:

1. **Tell one person.** It is the hardest step and the one that changes most.
2. **Put something in the way.** Self-exclusion, blocking software and a block on your bank card each take a few minutes and work while your willpower is asleep. [How to stop gambling](/articles/how-to-stop-gambling) goes through them.
3. **Talk to someone who does this for a living.** It is free and confidential, and nobody will be shocked. The numbers are on [our help page](/help).

You do not have to be sure you have a problem before you ask. Being unsure is reason enough.

## The short version

- There is no threshold of money lost. The signs are about control, honesty and consequences.
- Two questions catch most problems early: needing to bet more and more, and lying about it.
- Chasing losses is the clearest warning sign of all.
- It counts whatever it is called — betting, trading, a game.
- You do not need to hit bottom, and you do not need to be certain, to ask for help.
