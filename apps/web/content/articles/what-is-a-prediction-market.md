A prediction market is a place where people trade on the answer to a question about the future. Will the central bank cut rates next month? Will this candidate win? Will a film take more than a hundred million on its opening weekend? Each question has a price, the price moves as people buy and sell, and at any moment that price can be read as the crowd's estimate of how likely the thing is to happen.

That last part is the point. A prediction market is less a way to bet than a way to *measure* — to turn a lot of scattered opinions into one number.

## How a prediction market works

Most markets ask a yes-or-no question and sell two kinds of share: **yes** and **no**. When the question is settled, the winning share pays a fixed amount — say one dollar, or one point — and the losing share pays nothing.

Because a winning share is worth exactly one, the price of a share before the result is known behaves like a probability:

| A “yes” share costs | The market is saying |
| --- | --- |
| 0.90 | about a 90% chance |
| 0.50 | a coin toss |
| 0.07 | very unlikely, but not impossible |

If you think the true chance is higher than the price, you buy. If you think it is lower, you sell, or buy the other side. Your buying pushes the price up; somebody else's selling pushes it down. The price settles where the people willing to back “yes” and the people willing to back “no” meet.

Nobody sets that number. It is what is left over when everyone who disagreed with it has acted.

## Why the price is often a good forecast

Three things make a market price better than most single opinions.

**It adds up information nobody holds alone.** One person knows the polling, another knows the local mood, a third has read the legal filings. Each trades on what they know, and the price ends up reflecting all of it.

**It weights confidence.** Someone who is sure trades more than someone who is guessing. A survey counts every answer once; a market lets conviction show.

**It rewards being right rather than being loud.** There is no prize for an entertaining forecast. The only way to come out ahead is to be closer to the truth than the price was.

The best-known evidence comes from the Iowa Electronic Markets, run by the University of Iowa since 1988 as a research and teaching project. Its researchers compared the market's forecast with 964 national polls across five US presidential elections, from 1988 to 2004. The market was closer to the final result about three times in four, and the gap was widest months before polling day.

The economists Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz reviewed that record and others in 2004 and found a similar pattern in sport, in company sales forecasts and in film takings: market prices were usually accurate, and beat most of the benchmarks they were compared with. Not everyone reads the evidence so generously — other researchers have argued that polls, read carefully, do about as well. The fair summary is that a busy market is a very good forecaster and not a magic one.

## What a price does not tell you

A market price is an estimate, and it is worth being clear about its limits.

**70% is not “will happen”.** Things priced at 70% should fail to happen about three times in ten. When one of them does fail, the market was not necessarily wrong — it may have been exactly right about the odds. You can only judge a forecaster over many forecasts. We wrote more about this in [what a 70% chance really means](/articles/how-to-read-prediction-market-odds).

**Thin markets are noisy.** A question that a handful of people have traded can be moved a long way by one of them. Prices on popular questions with a lot of money behind them deserve more trust than prices on obscure ones.

**Long shots are overpriced.** People like cheap tickets with big payouts, so outcomes with a 1% or 2% chance tend to trade a little above their true value. The same bias shows up at racecourses, where it has a name: the favourite–longshot bias.

**A price is not quite a probability.** The economist Charles Manski pointed out that a price reflects traders' beliefs *and* how much they are willing to risk. In practice the two usually sit close together, but it is a reason to read 63% as “roughly three in five” and not as a measurement to two decimal places.

**Markets can be wrong together.** If everybody is working from the same mistaken assumption, the price inherits it. A market aggregates what people believe. It cannot conjure knowledge nobody has.

## Real money and play money

Prediction markets come in two kinds, and the difference matters more than anything else on this page.

In a **real-money market** you buy shares with money and are paid in money. The prices tend to be sharp, because being wrong costs something. It also means that, whatever it is called, the experience is close to betting: you can lose what you put in, the next market is always one tap away, and the pull to win it back is the same pull. Whether such markets are legal, and for whom, varies a great deal from one country to the next.

In a **play-money market** you trade with points. You might expect the forecasts to be much worse. The best-known comparison says otherwise: over a season of American football in 2003, a play-money market forecast the results about as well as a real-money one. What drives people, it turns out, is largely the scoreboard and the satisfaction of being right. And nothing leaves your bank account, whatever happens.

## Why bother, if there is no money in it?

Because forecasting is a skill, and one worth having.

Committing to a number — not “I reckon he'll win” but “I think it is 60%” — and then finding out what happened is a quick way to learn how well you actually understand something. Do it for a few months and you start to notice your own habits: overconfidence about things you care about, a weakness for long shots, a reluctance to change your mind when the evidence moves.

That is useful well beyond the game. It is, more or less, what good judgement is.

This site exists to offer that part on its own. The questions and prices here come from real markets, the odds are exact — one divided by the probability, with nothing taken out — and the points cannot be bought, sold or cashed in. If you would like to see how your judgement holds up, you can start without signing up for anything.

## The short version

- A prediction market sells shares in the answer to a question; the price of a share is the crowd's estimate of the chance.
- Prices are often accurate because they combine many people's information and reward being right.
- They are estimates: 70% still fails three times in ten, thin markets are noisy, and long shots are overpriced.
- Real-money markets carry the same risks as betting. Play-money markets forecast about as well and cannot cost you anything.
