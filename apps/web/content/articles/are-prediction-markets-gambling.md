It depends who is asking, and why.

The people who run prediction markets say they are financial exchanges. State gambling regulators say they are sportsbooks without a licence. Economists say they are a useful way of finding out what is likely to happen. All three have a point, because “is it gambling?” is really three questions wearing one coat: what does the law say, how does the thing work, and what does it do to the person using it.

The third is the one that matters if you are deciding whether to use one. But the first two explain why the argument is so loud.

## Part one: the law

In the United States, real-money prediction markets operate as exchanges overseen by the federal derivatives regulator, the CFTC. A contract that pays a dollar if a team wins is, on this view, an “event contract”: the same kind of instrument as a future on the price of wheat. Federal law governs it, and state gambling law does not apply.

State regulators see a bet on a football match, offered to anyone aged 18 with a phone, in states where a sportsbook must be licensed and taxed and, in most of them, closed to anyone under 21. More than a dozen states have taken action against the exchanges.

The courts have not settled it. They have split:

- In **April 2026**, a federal appeals court in Philadelphia ruled that New Jersey could not apply its gambling laws to Kalshi's sports contracts.
- In **August 2026**, the appeals court in San Francisco ruled that Nevada could.
- In **September 2026**, the appeals court in Cincinnati ruled that Ohio and Tennessee could.

When appeals courts disagree like this, the usual next stop is the Supreme Court. Until then, whether a sports contract on a prediction market is legally “gambling” depends on where in the country you are standing.

Two things follow that are worth holding on to. First, “it's legal” and “it's regulated as finance” tell you which office supervises the company. They tell you nothing about what the product does to its customers. Second, the fight is mostly about sport, for a reason: sport is now the most traded subject on both of the big platforms by a large margin. In June and July 2026 alone, during the World Cup, around 80 billion dollars of sports contracts changed hands across the two. Whatever these sites were founded to be, most of what happens on them today is betting on games.

## Part two: how it works

Here the markets have a real case, and it is worth being fair to it.

At a sportsbook you bet against the house. The house sets the price, builds a margin into it, and makes its money when you lose. It can — and does — limit or close the accounts of customers who win too often. ([The bookmaker's margin, explained](/articles/bookmaker-margin-explained) covers how.)

A prediction market is different in each of those respects:

- **You trade against other people.** The operator matches buyers with sellers and takes a fee. It does not need you to lose.
- **Nobody sets the price.** It moves to wherever buyers and sellers agree, which is why it can be read as a probability. ([What a prediction market is](/articles/what-is-a-prediction-market) explains this.)
- **You can sell before the end.** A position can be closed at the current price, the way a share can.
- **Winning is allowed.** The market has no reason to ban a skilful trader. Skilful traders are what make the price accurate.

Those are not small differences, and they are why economists take these markets seriously. A well-traded market gathers scattered information into one number, and that number has often proved a better forecast than polls or pundits. That is a public good, and you can use it without ever placing a trade.

But notice what the list does not say. It does not say the average participant makes money. Before fees, every dollar one trader wins is a dollar another loses. After fees, the total is negative. And the dollars do not move at random: they move from people trading on a hunch after work to people who do it for a living with data and software. A fairer structure is not the same thing as a better result for an ordinary person. A poker room does not have a house edge on the cards either.

## Part three: what it is like for the person

Put the paperwork aside and describe the activity. You put money at risk on an outcome you do not control, hoping to get back more than you put in. You find out quickly. You can do it again at once, from your phone, at any hour, on hundreds of events a day.

That is the definition of gambling that matters for harm, and it is the one the main American body for problem gambling uses. Its position is that trading event contracts is “functionally gambling, regardless of how it is legally defined”, and that the same protections should apply: clear information about the risk, limits that people can set, access to help, and a minimum age of 21.

The features that make gambling risky are all present:

- **Speed.** Outcomes in minutes, in the case of in-game contracts, and no pause before the next one.
- **Losses that invite chasing.** The pull to win back what was just lost is the single most recognisable sign of a gambling problem, and nothing about an exchange removes it.
- **The feeling of skill.** Charts, order books and the word “trading” make it easy to believe that this is analysis rather than betting, which is exactly the belief that keeps people going after they should have stopped. ([The gambler's fallacy, near misses and other tricks of the mind](/articles/gamblers-fallacy-and-near-misses) has more on that.)
- **Real money, without the usual brakes.** A self-exclusion signed with your state's sportsbooks generally does not cover these apps. For somebody in recovery, they can be a door back in that nobody thought to lock.

None of that makes prediction markets wicked, any more than a sportsbook is wicked. It makes them gambling, in the sense that counts when you are deciding whether they are safe for you.

## What about play money?

Take the money out and the answer changes, because two of the three ingredients of gambling go with it: nothing of value is staked, and nothing of value can be won.

Forecasting sites that score you for accuracy, and play-money markets whose currency cannot be cashed out, are games. Good ones are more than that: they are practice at judging uncertainty, and the research suggests they can be about as accurate as the real-money kind.

Two cautions, because this site is one of them and should not flatter itself.

First, some play-money sites sell extra play money. If it can never be turned back into cash there is still nothing to win, so it is still not gambling in the legal sense. But the money you paid was real. If you ever find yourself paying to win back points you lost, treat it exactly as you would treat chasing anywhere else: as a signal to stop.

Second, for some people in recovery, odds and stakes of *any* kind are a trigger, whatever is being staked. If a play-money slip gives you the same itch as a real one, it is not a safe substitute for you, and the honest advice is to leave it alone.

## So should you use one?

- **To find out what is likely to happen:** yes. Read the prices. It is free and it is the best thing these markets do.
- **To practise forecasting or enjoy the game:** use one with no money in it.
- **To trade with real money:** understand that you are gambling, against people who are better at it than you, with fewer protections than a licensed sportsbook would have to give you. If that is still what you want, set a limit before you start. Our [guide to the main sites](/articles/best-prediction-markets) says what each is for.
- **If you are trying to stop betting, or have ever had a problem with it:** no. The label is different and the risk is the same. [How to stop gambling](/articles/how-to-stop-gambling) is a better next page than any market.

## The short version

- Legally, US courts disagree, and the question is probably headed for the Supreme Court.
- Mechanically, a prediction market is fairer than a sportsbook: no house, no built-in margin, no ban on winners.
- For the person risking money, it is gambling, with the same hooks and often with weaker protections.
- Most of the trading is now on sport.
- Without money, it is a game — and still not the right game for everyone.
