“Best” depends on what you want a prediction market for. People come to them for three quite different things: to trade with real money, to find out what is likely to happen, or to enjoy the game of calling it. No single site is best at all three, and the one that is best for the first can be the worst choice for somebody who is trying to bet less.

Two things to know about this guide before you rely on it. Nothing here is an affiliate link, and nobody paid to be included; the sites are named, not linked. And we are not neutral: this site is a play-money forecasting game, built for people who want the fun without the stake. We have tried to be fair to the others anyway.

**Checked in November 2026.** This is a fast-moving area. Legal status in particular can change in a week.

## If you want to trade with real money

Read this section with one sentence in mind, from the main US body for problem gambling: trading event contracts is “functionally gambling, regardless of how it is legally defined.” You are staking money on an uncertain outcome hoping to get more back. Whatever the paperwork calls it, the risks to you are the risks of betting.

**Kalshi** is an exchange overseen by the US derivatives regulator, the CFTC. You trade yes-or-no contracts priced between 1 and 99 cents, against other traders rather than against the house. It has by far the widest range of questions and has grown at an extraordinary rate, most of it in sport: sport is now the most traded subject on the site by a large margin. That growth is also why its legal position is unsettled (see below), and why what you can trade depends on the state you are in.

**Polymarket** is the best known internationally, and its prices on elections and world events are the ones newspapers quote. Its main site runs on a blockchain and settles in crypto dollars; it has been closed to US residents since a settlement with the regulator in 2022. In late 2025 it introduced a separate, CFTC-regulated service for Americans, which by July 2026 accounted for about two fifths of its trading. Several states are contesting it.

**PredictIt** is the long-running American site for political questions, with a cap on how much one person can put on any single question. It has spent the last few years in dispute and then agreement with the regulator and has been changing as a result, so check its current terms rather than trusting an old description, including this one.

**The Iowa Electronic Markets** are the original: run by the University of Iowa's business school since 1988 for teaching and research. Real money, but nobody can put in more than $500, and there are only ever a few markets. Its importance is the research it made possible, not the trading.

Stockbrokers and sportsbook brands have started offering event contracts inside their own apps as well. They are the same product with a different front door.

### Before putting money into any of them

- **Is it legal where you live?** In the US this is honestly unclear. Federal appeals courts have reached opposite answers: one ruled in April 2026 that New Jersey could not apply its gambling laws to Kalshi's sports contracts, another ruled in August that Nevada could, and a third ruled in September that Ohio and Tennessee could. The question may go to the Supreme Court. Outside the US, many countries block these sites or treat them as unlicensed betting.
- **It is not free to trade.** There is no bookmaker's margin, but there are fees, and a gap between the buying and selling price. Somebody trading casually against professionals and algorithms should expect to lose more than those costs.
- **The protections are thinner than at a licensed sportsbook.** A self-exclusion you signed with your state's sportsbooks generally does not cover these apps, and the minimum age is often 18 rather than 21. If you have excluded yourself from betting, this is a way back in, and you should treat it as one.
- **Only money you can lose.** The standard advice, and still the right one.

## If you want to know what is likely to happen

You do not need an account for this, or any money.

The prices on the big real-money markets are public, and reading them costs nothing. A contract trading at 70 cents is a crowd's estimate of a 70% chance, and for well-traded questions those estimates have a good record. If that is new, start with [what a prediction market is](/articles/what-is-a-prediction-market) and then [what a 70% chance really means](/articles/how-to-read-prediction-market-odds), which covers where prices tend to lean.

For most people this is the best use of a real-money prediction market: as something to read.

**Metaculus** is worth knowing here too. It is not a market at all. Thousands of people submit probabilities for questions about science, technology, health and world affairs, and the site combines them into one forecast and scores everybody on how they did. Nobody stakes anything. On long-range and technical questions, where markets are thin, it is often the best estimate available.

## If you want to play, practise and get better

This is where money stops being necessary, and the evidence says it stops being very useful as well. When researchers compared a real-money market with a play-money one on the same 208 football games, the two were about equally accurate. What makes a market good is plenty of people trying to be right. A reputation or a leaderboard will do that as well as a wallet.

**Manifold** is the liveliest play-money site. Anyone can create a question on anything, which makes it both a serious forecasting tool and a very odd place. Its currency cannot be converted to cash. It ran real-money sweepstakes markets for a while and closed them in 2025, though it still holds occasional prize drawings.

**Good Judgment Open** grew out of the research project that identified “superforecasters”. It runs forecasting challenges, mostly on international affairs, and scores you against everybody else. It is free, and it is the closest thing there is to a gym for this skill.

**This site** takes its questions and live odds from real markets and lets you forecast them with points. The odds are exactly fair, with no margin at any number of picks; there are daily limits and breaks you can set; and a reality check shows your record beside what it would have cost at a bookmaker. It is deliberately a smaller thing than the others. It is for people who want the game without the stake.

## How to choose

- **You are trying to bet less, or have had a problem with gambling.** Stay away from the real-money ones entirely, whatever they are called. If odds and stakes of any kind stir things up, stay away from the play-money ones as well, including this one, and look at [how to stop gambling](/articles/how-to-stop-gambling) instead.
- **You want the best available forecast of something.** Read the big markets' prices and Metaculus. Trade nothing.
- **You want to get better at judging probabilities.** Good Judgment Open or Metaculus, where you are scored and can see your calibration.
- **You enjoy calling sport, politics or the news and want that to be all it is.** A play-money site.
- **You want to trade with money and you understand it is gambling.** A regulated exchange, in a place where it is clearly legal, with an amount you can afford to lose, and with a limit you set before you start. We have written separately about [whether prediction markets are gambling](/articles/are-prediction-markets-gambling).

## The short version

- No one site is best. Decide whether you want to trade, to read forecasts, or to play.
- Real-money prediction markets are, for the person using them, gambling. Their legal status in the US is being fought over in the courts right now.
- You can get most of their value by reading the prices, for free.
- Play-money and scored forecasting sites are about as accurate, and nobody loses their rent.
- If you are stepping back from betting, the real-money ones are a way back in. Treat them that way.
