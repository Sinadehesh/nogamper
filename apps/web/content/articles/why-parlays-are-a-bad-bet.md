A parlay is several bets joined into one. Every pick has to win, and in return the payouts multiply. Depending on where you live it is called a parlay, an accumulator, an acca or a multi; when all the picks come from one match it is a same-game parlay.

It is the bet a sportsbook most wants you to place. It gets the banner at the top of the app, the boosted price, the ready-made slip. There is a simple reason for that, and it is arithmetic rather than conspiracy: a parlay is the one product where the bookmaker's fee is charged several times over on a single stake, and where almost nobody notices.

## Multiplying the price multiplies the fee

Start with one ordinary bet. A point-spread bet is designed to be a coin toss, and a fair price for a coin toss is 2.00: stake 1, get 2 back. The sportsbook offers 1.91 instead. The gap is its margin, about 4.5% of what you stake. (If that is new, [the bookmaker's margin, explained](/articles/bookmaker-margin-explained) goes through it slowly.)

Now put three of those bets on one slip.

| | Fair price | Sportsbook's price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One pick | 2.00 | 1.91 |
| Three picks, multiplied | 8.00 | 6.97 |

All three will win one time in eight. A fair payout for a one-in-eight shot is 8.00. You are being offered 6.97. Over many such slips you get back 87 cents for every dollar staked, not 95.5.

You did not agree to a higher fee. You agreed to the same fee three times, and fees that are multiplied together compound:

| Picks on the slip | What comes back, on average | What the sportsbook keeps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 95.5% | 4.5% |
| 2 | 91.2% | 8.8% |
| 3 | 87.1% | 12.9% |
| 5 | 79.4% | 20.6% |
| 8 | 69.2% | 30.8% |
| 10 | 63.1% | 36.9% |

A ten-pick slip at standard prices is a worse deal than most slot machines. Nothing on the slip says so. The number that grows on the screen as you add picks is the payout; the number that grows at the same rate, out of sight, is the price of the ticket.

## “But the payout is huge”

It is, and that is the other half of the design.

Take ten coin-toss picks. A fair payout would be 1,024 times the stake; the sportsbook's is about 646. Either way the slip wins roughly once in a thousand tries. What happens on the other 999 matters more than it looks:

- about one slip in six gets **seven, eight or nine** picks right, and loses;
- about one in a hundred gets nine out of ten, and loses.

Each of those is a full loss, the same as getting none right. It does not feel like one. It feels like nearly winning a fortune, and “nearly winning” is among the best-studied hooks in gambling: in brain-imaging experiments, near misses were rated as more unpleasant than ordinary losses and yet left people keener to play again. A long slip manufactures them by the dozen. There is more on that in [the gambler's fallacy, near misses and other tricks of the mind](/articles/gamblers-fallacy-and-near-misses).

So the parlay gives the customer a large number to look at, a steady supply of “so close”, and a small stake that makes each loss easy to shrug off. It gives the sportsbook several margins for the price of one.

## Same-game parlays are harder still to check

With picks from different matches you can at least do the multiplication yourself. With picks from the *same* match you cannot, because the picks are connected. If a team wins by a lot, its striker probably scored; those are not two independent events, and multiplying their prices would be wrong.

The sportsbook therefore prices the combination itself, with its own model of how the pieces fit together, and shows you one final number. You have no way of working out what a fair price would have been, which means you have no way of seeing the margin. A fee you cannot calculate is not likely to be a small one.

## What sportsbooks actually keep

This is not only theory. Regulators publish what was staked and what was kept.

**Nevada** has the longest record. In 2025 its sportsbooks kept 7.45% of all the money bet on sports — and 32.59% of the money bet on parlay cards. That is not a freak year. In the ten years to 2025 the share kept on parlay cards ranged from 14.5% to 40.2%, while the share kept on football never rose above 8%. In every one of the 34 years the state has broken the figures down by sport, parlays were the line on which the house kept the largest share. (One caveat: Nevada's “parlay” line counts parlay cards specifically. Multi-pick bets placed in apps are folded into each sport's total.)

**Illinois** reports parlays placed online directly. In the year to June 2026, they made up three in every five sports bets placed in the state, almost 200 million of them. They accounted for nearly 62% of everything bettors lost: 879 million dollars.

Read those two together. The bet that is promoted hardest is the bet people place most often, and it is the one that costs them the most.

## Boosts, insurance and other sweeteners

“Parlay boost: 30% extra winnings.” “Money back if one leg lets you down.”

These are real, and sometimes they do improve the price of a particular slip. But look at what they are attached to. An offer that only applies to slips of four picks or more is an offer to move you from a bet with a 4.5% fee to one with a fee of 17% or more. A boost that hands back part of that is still a good trade for the house. The offer exists to change which product you buy, and it usually comes with a clock on it.

## Is a parlay ever a reasonable bet?

If a tiny stake on a long slip is a bit of fun on a Saturday, and you know what it costs, that is your call. People buy lottery tickets on the same terms. The trouble starts when the cost is invisible, or when the slip is a way of trying to turn a little money into a lot because a lot is needed. A parlay is the worst available tool for that job.

Two honest rules of thumb:

- **The more picks, the worse the price.** Every time, at every sportsbook.
- **If you would not place each pick on its own, do not place them together.** Joining them adds no information. It adds only fee.

## What a fair multi-pick slip looks like

On this site you can put up to five questions on one slip, and the combined odds are exactly the fair odds multiplied together, because there is no margin on any of them to compound. A slip of five costs the same, per point staked, as a slip of one: nothing. Beside what the slip would pay, it shows the chance that it comes in, so the long shot is never dressed up as anything else. And the points are only points.

That is not generosity. It is just what the arithmetic looks like when nobody is being paid by it.

## The short version

- A parlay multiplies the payout and the bookmaker's margin together. Three picks roughly triple the fee; ten take more than a third of everything staked.
- Long slips lose almost every time, and usually by “just one leg”, which is the feeling that brings people back.
- Same-game parlays are priced by the sportsbook's own model, so the fee cannot even be checked.
- The published figures agree: parlays are where sportsbooks keep the most and bettors lose the most.
- Adding picks never adds value. It adds cost.
